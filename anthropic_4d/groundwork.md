# Software Architect Learning Plan — Goal Groundwork

> Purpose: defines the goal and constraints for a structured learning plan that
> prepares an experienced developer to move into a software architecture role.
> This is groundwork only; the plan itself is to be designed later.

## Current baseline

- Experienced software developer, hands-on within the AWS ecosystem for the past year.
- Has participated in real infrastructure decisions: ECS vs Lambda, logs vs metrics,
  queues vs topics, sync vs async.
- Practical exposure to infrastructure-as-code, deployment, and monitoring
  ("click ops" included).
- Writes application code that runs in the cloud; operates mostly within a single
  team's scope.

## Target outcome

- Primary direction: Cloud / Infrastructure Architect. Open to shifting toward
  Solutions or Technical Architect as progress reveals fit — the plan must not be
  invalidated by such a pivot.
- Motivation: career advancement (securing architect-level roles).
- Success criterion: proven capability — the ability to design and defend a real
  system end-to-end, validated by (a) a real production system designed/defended at
  work and (b) a documented architecture case study as fallback proof. No
  certification is the success metric.

## Timeline

- 6 months of core work, externally anchored to a project's due date; the individual
  aims to be ready for new roles by then.
- Ancillary topics may extend beyond the 6-month window.
- Pacing must assume ~6 months of *available* effort, not 6 months of calendar time,
  because diversions are expected.

## Constraints

- A real production system is available to tinker with, but the individual holds no
  unilateral authority over team architecture decisions.
- At times, others will perform the architecture work, or urgent priorities will
  divert the team; the plan must accommodate both.
- Capability must therefore be proven through influence, design, and defense — not
  command.

## What a successful plan looks like

- Outcome-anchored, not content-anchored: each phase culminates in demonstrable
  capability (a decision defended, a design authored), not completion of a course.
- Builds on the existing AWS baseline, pushing from infra decision-maker toward
  architect-level scope (cross-service trade-offs, cost, reliability, org-wide
  patterns).
- Resilient to non-architect work: phases include buffers and deferrable markers so
  urgent diversions do not break the trajectory.
- Dual proof modes:
  - Lead mode: own and defend a real design on the production system.
  - Contribute mode: when team priorities divert, author a thorough design +
    trade-off analysis (case study) as fallback evidence even if others implement.
- Explicit "influence without authority" competency: RFC/proposal writing, cost/risk
  framing, stakeholder defense.
- Capstone: a real work system design plus a written case study as the proof artifact.
- Dual-track by design: a 6-month core track plus an explicit ancillary backlog for
  post-6-month extension.
- Self-validating checkpoints: clear "ready to defend this" gates in the absence of a
  certification metric.
- Allows the specialization to shift (Cloud/Infra → Solutions/Technical) without
  invalidating the plan.

## Why this matters (importance)

- The 6-month deadline is real (project due date → role readiness), so pacing must be
  disciplined, not open-ended.
- "Proven capability" without a certification means the plan must manufacture
  evidence, not just knowledge — the difference between having learned architecture
  and being hirable as an architect.
- Existing decision-making experience is the lever: the plan should convert scattered
  infra decisions into a coherent, defensible architectural practice rather than
  starting from zero.
- Limited authority means proof leans more on demonstrable artifacts (the case study)
  than on shipped team decisions; the artifact must be guaranteed produced even when
  sidelined.
- Diversions are guaranteed, so buffer is a core requirement, not a nice-to-have.
