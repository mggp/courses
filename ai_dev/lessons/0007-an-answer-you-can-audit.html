An answer you can audit

Six lessons, one run. Documents in, a cited answer out — and two numbers that tell you whether to trust it.

~45 minutes  ·  Python  ·  lesson 6  ·  glossary

Why this lesson

This is the mission's success criterion, built: documents in, retrieved context, grounded answers out — with citations the user can verify. Nothing here is new machinery; every piece was built in lessons 1–6. What is new is the wiring: one pipeline that runs recall, rerank, generation, and evaluation together — and the discovery that a clean citation is not the same as a faithful answer. At a fintech, that distinction is the difference between an auditable answer and a confident, correctly-footnoted lie.

The whole spine

Indexchunk + embed (1–2) → Recalldense + BM25 + RRF (3) → Rerankcross-encoder (6) → Generateanswer with citations → Evaluaterecall@k + faithfulness (2, 5)

The only new stage is generate with citations: the prompt hands the model its retrieved chunks — each labelled with its id — and instructs it to answer only from them and cite every claim, like [p3]. That citation is provenance made visible: the trail from claim to chunk that a reader (or an auditor) can follow.

Citations are not proof

Here is the capstone's one real insight, and the reason the pipeline runs two checks, not one. A citation is a pointer — it says "this chunk was retrieved and I'm pointing at it." It says nothing about whether the claim is actually supported by that chunk. A model can cite a real chunk and assert something the chunk never says.

  • Structural citation integrity — does every [pN] point at a chunk that was actually retrieved? This is the local sanity check you built: necessary, but weak.
  • Faithfulness — does any retrieved chunk actually entail each claim? (The NLI check from lesson 5, now pointed at the retrieved chunks.)

The field's stronger name for the whole problem is attribution. It asks whether each claim points at the specific source that supports it. The citation benchmark ALCE splits this into citation recall (does every claim have a citation?) and citation precision (does every cited chunk entail its claim?). The harness has the structural floor plus faithfulness; the natural next upgrade is per-citation faithfulness — check a claim against the chunk it cites, not merely against any chunk in the top-k.

One gotcha worth knowing. The NLI cross-encoder from lesson 5 has a short maximum input length. Feed it a claim against all three chunks concatenated and it labels near-verbatim matches "neutral" — the premise is too long. The fix: check the claim against each chunk individually and count it supported if any chunk entails it. That also gives you a nicer answer: "supported by p5", not just "supported".

Build it

In exercises/lesson0007/, capstone.py consolidates lessons 1–6 into one file. The gold set carries a canned answer per question (with citations and hand-written atomic claims) so the whole thing runs offline; --live regenerates answers and claims with your own LLM.

  1. Run uv sync, then uv run capstone.py. You should see mean recall@3 = 1.00 and mean faithfulness = 0.92.
  2. Read the recall column. Every row is 1.00 — including the paraphrase question that beat every retrieval trick in lessons 2–4. Lessons 1–6 got retrieval to the point where the gold chunk always makes the top-3. That column is now solved; stop tuning it.
  3. Read the citations. Each answer cites its chunk, and every citation is marked ok — it points at a chunk that was actually retrieved. This is structural citation integrity, the citation trail the mission promised: a reader can open corpus.md and inspect the alleged source. Do not confuse it with attribution yet.
  4. Read the catch. Question 6, "What method keeps long-term recall sharp?". The answer's first claim — spaced repetition — is supported by p5. Its second claim — "the intervals are fixed at one day, three days, and one week" — is not in context, despite carrying the [p5] citation. Faithfulness 1/2 = 0.50. The structural check passed; faithfulness caught it. In ALCE's vocabulary, checking that second claim specifically against p5 would be a citation-precision / attribution check.
  5. Confirm the retrieval is not the point. That question's recall@3 is 1.00 and its citation is structurally valid — by every retrieval and provenance measure, the answer looks perfect. Only faithfulness sees the lie. A correctly-footnoted hallucination is the failure mode that ends careers in a compliance shop; per-citation attribution is the sharper production version of this check.
  6. (Optional) Make it real. Run uv run capstone.py --live — the LLM writes the answers and decomposes its own claims, then the same harness audits them. Compare its citations and faithfulness to the canned set.

Primary sources for this lesson

The capstone synthesizes the RAG paper (Lewis et al., 2020), whose "provenance" is the citation trail you built, and RAGAS (Es et al., 2023), whose faithfulness recipe caught question 6's lie. The two new primary sources name the stronger citation problem: "Attributed Question Answering" (Bohnet et al., 2022) defines attribution as the ability to source generated text, while ALCE (Gao et al., EMNLP 2023) benchmarks citation quality. Its finding is worth absorbing: even the best systems lacked complete citation support 50% of the time on ELI5.

Key terms: provenance, citation, attribution, faithfulness, recall@k.
