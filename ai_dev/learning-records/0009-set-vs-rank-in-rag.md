# Set vs rank: why RAG measures sets, not orderings

The learner reasoned his way to a distinction that sharpens lesson 6's "reranking fixes ordering" shorthand: a generator reads a *set* of chunks, so rank is irrelevant within the top-k and only matters where the k cut falls. Reranking's real value in RAG is re-selecting which chunks cross that cut out of a larger recall pool — not polishing order. "Just raise k" is the alternative lever, paid for in tokens, latency, lost-in-the-middle attention, and faithfulness risk (more junk to blend). The metric consequence: recall@k / precision@k (set metrics) are the right lens for RAG; MRR / NDCG are search-heritage metrics to *translate*, not to adopt.

**Evidence**: The learner's own hypotheses — "would reranking matter more in systems where rank is important? Are RAG pipelines usually an example?" and "rank itself is not all that relevant" — plus his correct unprompted parsing that BM25 is not a bi-encoder, and that RRF's role ends at pool formation.

**Implications**: Teach retrieval metrics as a taxonomy with a decision rule — *RAG → set metrics* — and report recall@k, precision@k, and faithfulness in the capstone, not MRR. When reading reranking papers (which quote MRR@10 / NDCG), translate them into recall terms for the learner's pipeline. Keep lesson 6's pool-size story ("recall is the floor" at N=3, "reranker has its own blind spots" at N=24) as the empirical anchor for this. "Lost in the Middle" (Liu et al., TACL 2023, arXiv:2307.03172) is the caveat that keeps rank from being fully irrelevant at large k.
