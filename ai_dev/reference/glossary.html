Glossary

The canonical language of this course. Every lesson uses these terms and only these terms. Definitions grow as your understanding does.

The retrieval pipeline

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation): A pattern where a model answers a question by first pulling relevant excerpts from a document store, then generating a response conditioned on those excerpts. Avoid: "talking to your documents", "AI over your data".

Chunking: Splitting a source document into small pieces that can be retrieved independently. Chunk boundaries affect recall, precision, faithfulness, citation granularity, and context cost. Avoid: "splitting", "segmentation".

Fixed-size chunking: Cutting a document by a token or word budget, usually with overlap between adjacent chunks. It is predictable and inexpensive, but a boundary can split a definition, table, or argument. Use it as a reproducible baseline, not a universal optimum.

Semantic chunking: Placing chunk boundaries where sentence or topic meaning changes, often using a model-derived distance or similarity threshold. It can preserve coherent units, but adds processing cost, parameter sensitivity, and a need for a maximum chunk size. Avoid: treating "semantic" as a quality guarantee.

Embedding: An array of numbers produced by an embedding model that maps text into a vector space where similar meaning sits nearby. Avoid: "vectorization" when you mean the model output.

Cosine similarity: The cosine of the angle between two vectors, used as a measure of how close two embeddings are; 1 means identical direction, 0 means orthogonal. Avoid: "score" without saying of what.

Retrieval: The step that selects evidence for a query. It may use keyword scoring, vector similarity, graph traversal, or a measured combination of these; dense retrieval is the vector-specific case.

Generation: The step that sends the retrieved chunks plus the question to a language model and gets back a grounded answer.

Grounding: Anchoring an answer in source material the model can be checked against, rather than its internal knowledge. Avoid: "making it factual".

Provenance: The identity of the chunk an answer came from, carried on every chunk (an id, later a source path). Retrieval without provenance is an answer without a citation. Avoid: "metadata" when you mean the citation trail.

Citation: A marker in a generated answer naming the chunk a claim came from (e.g. [p3]) — provenance made visible to the reader. A valid citation proves only that the chunk was retrieved, not that the claim is faithful; a correctly-cited lie passes the citation check and is caught only by faithfulness. Avoid: "reference" when you mean the chunk itself.

Attribution: The property that each claim in a generated answer points to the specific source that supports it — provenance asserted per claim, not just per answer. The umbrella term for citation quality, measured two ways: citation recall (every claim cited) and citation precision (every citation actually entails its claim). A citation at a retrieved-but-non-supporting chunk passes the structural check yet fails attribution. From Bohnet et al., 2022; benchmarked by ALCE (Gao et al., 2023). Avoid: "citations" when you mean the stronger claim that they support the text.

Gold answer: A hand-written pair of a question and the exact sentence that must be retrieved to answer it. The unit you score retrieval against.

Recall@k: The fraction of gold chunks found in the top-k retrieved chunks, measured at the k you will actually feed the generator. 1.0 on an index smaller than k is vacuous.

Precision@k: The fraction of the top-k retrieved chunks that are actually relevant — the signal-to-noise ratio of what reaches the generator. RAGAS calls this context precision. The mirror of recall@k: recall asks whether we got everything relevant (completeness), precision asks how much of what we sent was noise (purity). The two trade off against each other across k. Avoid: quoting precision without saying how "relevant" was decided (gold chunk vs. LLM-judged).

MRR@k (Mean Reciprocal Rank): A ranking metric — for each query, the reciprocal of the rank of its first correct result (1/rank), capped at k (a miss beyond k counts 0), averaged over queries. Rewards getting the right chunk to rank 1 far more than to rank 5; unlike recall@k it distinguishes "in the top-k" from "at the top". The metric reranking papers quote, because reranking's whole job is to raise rank. Avoid: quoting an MRR as a percentage improvement without saying it is relative.

NDCG (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain): A ranking metric that scores the whole list — each result's gain (relevance, often graded 0–3) is summed with a log discount for position, then normalized by the ideal (best-possible) ordering. Unlike MRR it rewards multiple relevant results; unlike recall/precision it rewards order and graded relevance. The search/recommender metric; rarely the right lens for RAG, where a generator reads a set rather than scans a list. Avoid: "NDCG" when you only mean "top result was right" — that is MRR.

Harness: The runnable measurement rig — corpus loader, chunker, retrieval system, gold set, and recall@k scorer wired into one script (evaluate.py, hybrid.py) that prints the verdict. Its job is to replace "seems fine" with a reproducible number. The name is borrowed from software testing, where a test harness runs a system under test with controlled inputs and reports pass/fail. Avoid: "the script" when you mean the whole rig; "benchmark" when you mean only the gold set plus metric without the runner.

Search

Dense search: Retrieval that embeds every chunk and the query into a fixed-length vector space and ranks by cosine similarity. Called "dense" because the vectors are fully populated (unlike a sparse vector). Avoid: "AI search".

Sparse vector: A mostly-zero vector — how keyword search represents a document, with a 1 at each present term's position. The contrast that gives dense search its name.

BM25: The classic keyword scorer: for each query term, an inverse-document-frequency weight times a capped term frequency, normalized by document length. Matches exact terms after analysis; blind to paraphrase. Avoid: "fuzzy search".

IDF (inverse document frequency): A per-term weight — log((N − df + 0.5)/(df + 0.5) + 1) — rewarding terms that appear in few documents. "Authentication" beats "the".

Stemming: Reducing inflected words to a common stem ("reviewed" → "review") so keyword search matches across forms. Avoid: "lemmatization" unless you mean dictionary-based normalization.

Analysis: The preprocessing a keyword search applies before matching: lowercasing, stopword removal, stemming.

Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF): Fusing ranked lists by adding 1/(k + rank) per list per document, so a chunk that ranks high in any list accumulates points. Never averages scores — their scales are incomparable.

Hybrid search: Running keyword and dense search for the same query and fusing the results, typically with RRF. Each system covers the other's blindness — and fusion can lose a good hit to noise, so it is measured, never trusted.

Graph database: A store that represents entities as nodes and their typed connections as edges, with properties attached to either. It makes explicit relationships, path constraints, and multi-hop traversal queryable. Avoid: assuming a graph can recover relationships that were never modeled or extracted.

Graph traversal: Following edges from one or more starting nodes while applying relationship types, filters, or hop limits. It retrieves explicit structural relationships rather than merely finding semantically similar text.

Vector-graph retrieval: Combining vector similarity with graph traversal, such as using a graph to constrain entities and vector search to find the source passages that support the relationship. This differs from keyword-plus-dense hybrid search.

Candidate pool: The top-N chunks the recall stage hands to the reranker. Reranking can only re-order what is in the pool — recall is the floor. Too small a pool drops the right chunk before the reranker sees it; too large a pool makes the reranker rank a long tail of junk it scores badly. Measured, not guessed.

Reranking: The second stage of retrieval — a cross-encoder re-orders the recall stage's candidate pool by reading each (query, passage) pair together. The canonical fix for ordering failures (right chunk ranked fifth, generator reads three), and the first tool to rescue the paraphrase row without touching the query. In RAG the generator reads a set, so the value is re-selecting which chunks cross the k cut, not fine-grained ordering — rank matters to a generator only where the cut falls. Its scores are uncalibrated — only the order matters — and over the whole corpus it can trade one row for another, so it is measured, never trusted. From Nogueira & Cho, 2019. Avoid: "rescoring" without noting the order is what changes.

Chunk layer: The pipeline layer where documents are cut into retrievable units. Ruled out as the fix for the paraphrase gap in lesson 2.

Retrieval layer: The pipeline layer where the search step finds chunks. Its fixes: hybrid search (lesson 3), reranking with a cross-encoder (lesson 6), swapping in a query–passage retriever.

Query layer: The pipeline layer where the question is processed before search. Its fix: query rewriting and hypothetical documents (HyDE) — lesson 4.

Query rewriting (Rewrite-Retrieve-Read): Prompting an LLM to rephrase the search query before retrieval, to bridge the gap between how users phrase questions and how the corpus phrases facts. From Ma et al., EMNLP 2023. Avoid: "rephrasing" without noting it happens before search.

Multi-query expansion (RAG-Fusion): Generating several rewritten queries, retrieving with each, and fusing the ranked lists with RRF. One strong variant can carry the fusion; weak variants add noise. From Rackauckas, 2024.

HyDE (hypothetical document embeddings): Generating a hypothetical answer passage with an LLM and retrieving by its embedding instead of the query's. The unreal details are filtered by the encoder's dense bottleneck; the passage supplies the corpus's vocabulary. From Gao et al., 2022.

Model vocabulary

Embedding model: A model trained to map text (and sometimes images) into a shared vector space. Avoid: "the encoder" unless you are discussing its internals.

Bi-encoder: A model that encodes a query and a passage separately into two vectors, compared afterward by cosine similarity. Fast because passage vectors are precomputed, but the two texts never see each other — similarity is all it can measure. This is what all-MiniLM-L6-v2 is.

Cross-encoder: A model that reads a query and a passage together as one concatenated input and outputs a single relationship score, attending token-to-token across both. Captures relations (entailment, relevance) that a bi-encoder cannot, but runs per pair, so it is used only on a small candidate set — for reranking the top-k, or for the NLI entailment check in faithfulness. Avoid: "the encoder" when you mean this vs. a bi-encoder.

Language model (LLM): A model that generates text given a prompt. In this course it is the generator in RAG, never the store of your data. Avoid: "the AI" when you mean a specific model.

Context window: The number of tokens a language model can take as input in one call — the reason retrieval must select a few chunks rather than dump a whole corpus in.

Token: A unit of text a model reads and writes — roughly a word fragment, not a word. WordPiece tokenizers call these units "word pieces": "embedding" is three pieces (em, ##bed, ##ding). Avoid: "word" when you mean token.

Hallucination: Confident output that does not match the source material or reality. RAG reduces — it does not eliminate — hallucination.

Agent systems and integration

Agent: An LLM-driven control loop that can choose actions, call tools, read observations, and continue or stop under application policy. Unlike a fixed workflow, the next step is selected dynamically. Avoid: calling every LLM feature an agent.

Workflow: An LLM-and-tool system whose path is mostly specified by application code. It is usually more predictable and easier to test than an agent, but less flexible when the number or shape of steps is unknown.

Tool: A schema-described callable capability that an agent can invoke to read data, perform computation, or change external state. Its input validation, output contract, permissions, timeout, and side effects belong to the application boundary. Avoid: treating a tool description as authorization.

Resource: Addressable context or data made available for a user or model to read. It is the passive-data counterpart to a tool; a policy document is a resource, while writing a ticket note is a tool call.

Prompt template: A reusable message or message pattern that shapes an interaction. It supplies instructions or context; it does not itself execute an operation or grant permission.

Skill: A reusable package of instructions and supporting material, typically a directory containing SKILL.md plus optional scripts, references, and assets. A skill teaches an agent how to perform a class of work; it is not a callable tool.

MCP (Model Context Protocol): An open protocol for connecting LLM applications to external data and capabilities. Its host/client/server model standardizes access to server-provided resources, prompts, and tools. It does not define the host's planning loop, memory store, or authorization policy. Avoid: “MCP is the agent.”

Observation: The result returned by a tool or other environment interaction, fed back into the agent loop so the next decision is based on ground truth rather than an assumed outcome.

Multi-agent system: Multiple agents coordinating on one task. Its defensible use case is a measured need for independent parallel work, context isolation, or separate responsibilities; it also adds token, coordination, latency, and debugging costs.

Web delivery and design

API framework: The application boundary that handles HTTP routing, request parsing, validation, serialization, errors, documentation, and often dependency/lifecycle hooks. It should expose the use case; it should not own provider-specific model logic or domain policy by accident.

FastAPI: A Python API framework built around standard type hints, Pydantic-backed validation, OpenAPI/JSON Schema documentation, and dependency injection, with an async-friendly API surface. Avoid: claiming that its feature set guarantees lower end-to-end latency for every workload.

Flask: A small, explicit, WSGI-oriented Python web framework with a broad extension ecosystem. It supports async views, but its WSGI worker model means async is not automatically a higher-concurrency or lower-latency deployment model.

WSGI (Web Server Gateway Interface): A synchronous Python server-to-application protocol. The application receives an environ dictionary and start_response callback, then returns an iterable of byte strings. It is centered on ordinary HTTP request and response handling.

ASGI (Asynchronous Server Gateway Interface): A Python server-to-application protocol with an async callable receiving scope, receive, and send. It represents HTTP, WebSocket, and lifecycle messages and supports concurrency for code that actually awaits non-blocking I/O.

SOLID: Five object-design principles—Single Responsibility, Open/Closed, Liskov Substitution, Interface Segregation, and Dependency Inversion—used here as diagnoses for seams that need independent change or testing. Avoid: reciting the acronym without naming the boundary it protects.

Dependency inversion: Keep high-level application logic dependent on a stable abstraction or port, while provider-specific adapters implement that port. It is the design principle; dependency injection is one mechanism for supplying the implementation.

Port: An application-owned interface for a role or capability that can change independently, such as generating model output, retrieving evidence, or recording an audit event. A port is small when a client depends only on the operations it needs, not when it has an arbitrary number of methods.

Deep module: A module that hides substantial useful work behind a relatively simple public interface. It overlaps with a small port because both value a simple client-facing contract, but Interface Segregation chooses which operations a client depends on, while deep-module design focuses on how much implementation complexity the interface hides.

Adapter: A boundary component that translates an external interface—such as a model SDK, vector store, or cloud API—into the application's internal port and translates its failures/results back into the internal contract.

Experience boundary: The explicit line between what you have built or operated, what you understand architecturally, and what you have only read or plan to investigate. A credible interview answer preserves this line instead of converting a quickstart into production experience.

Cloud delivery

Amazon Bedrock: An AWS managed service that provides access to foundation models and related generative-AI features. In this course, name the specific feature you used, such as model inference or Knowledge Bases, instead of treating “Bedrock” as one undifferentiated product.

Converse API: Amazon Bedrock Runtime's normalized message-inference operation. It accepts messages and optional inference, guardrail, tool, and metadata configuration, then returns model output plus fields such as stop reason, token usage, and latency metrics.

IAM (Identity and Access Management): AWS's system for identifying callers and deciding which actions they may perform on which resources under which conditions. IAM authorization is separate from a model's generated answer and from application-level faithfulness checks.

Inference profile: A Bedrock identifier that can represent a routing target for model inference across one or more Regions, or an application-defined grouping used to track model usage and cost. Treat its routing and IAM requirements as configuration to verify, not as an invisible property of a model name.

Cloud runtime: The managed execution boundary that receives an application request and performs model inference. Moving generation to a cloud runtime does not automatically move retrieval, authorization, evaluation, or response routing there; each responsibility must be named.

Foundation model: A model broadly trained on diverse data at scale and intended to support many downstream tasks. It may later be instruction-tuned, aligned, fine-tuned, or otherwise adapted. “Pre-trained” is not enough by itself, because task-specific models can also be pre-trained.

RBAC (role-based access control): An authorization model that assigns a role definition, which contains permissions, to a principal at a scope. It answers what a user, group, service principal, or managed identity may do where; it does not authenticate that principal.

Safety and sampling

Guardrail: A broad operational term for a control that constrains, checks, authorizes, measures, or routes an AI system at a particular boundary. It is not one universal mechanism or formal taxonomy. A prompt instruction can be one input to a guardrail design, but it is not enforcement by itself.

Constrain: Narrow the set of outputs or actions that are allowed before execution. Examples include a declared output schema, an enum, a tool allowlist, a tenant boundary, or a maximum iteration count. A prompt-only restriction is a soft constraint unless an external control enforces it.

Check: Evaluate a proposed input, output, claim, or action against a rule or evidence and return a pass/fail/review result. Schema validation, faithfulness checking, and permission checking are different checks; a check becomes a runtime guardrail when it gates what happens next.

Route: Select the next safe path after a check, such as proceed, retry, repair, fallback, refuse, or human review. needs_review is an explicit route for uncertainty or risk.

Control plane: The policies, configuration, identity and approvals, runtime guardrails, measurements, audit events, rollout decisions, and escalation paths that govern an AI system. It surrounds the data-plane work—retrieval, inference, tool execution, and response delivery—rather than asking the model to enforce every rule on its own.

Measurement: An observed signal about system behavior or risk, such as unsupported-claim rate, false-accept rate, latency, cost, authorization denials, or human overrides. A measurement becomes a control-plane input when a defined threshold triggers routing, an alert, recalibration, a configuration change, rollout, or rollback; otherwise it is telemetry or reporting.

Fail closed: When a required check fails, the system performs no consequential action by default. It may abstain, explain the failed boundary, or escalate to a human instead of silently proceeding.

Abstention: An explicit decision not to answer or act automatically because evidence, confidence, authorization, or another required condition is insufficient. It is a controlled outcome, not an exception that should be hidden.

Structured output: Model output constrained to a declared schema, such as an object with typed fields for a decision, claims, citations, and uncertainty. Schema validation can be a runtime guardrail when it gates the next step; it checks shape, not truth.

Temperature: A sampling control that rescales model logits before probabilities are computed. Lower values sharpen the distribution toward likely tokens; higher values flatten it and permit more variation. It changes generation variability, not authorization or truth.

Top-p (nucleus sampling): A sampling control that keeps the smallest set of highest-probability tokens whose cumulative probability reaches p, then samples within that dynamic set. Lower p cuts more of the low-probability tail; it is not a safety filter.

Evaluation

Faithfulness: The fraction of an answer's atomic claims that are supported by the retrieved context — supported claims / total claims. Measures the text the model wrote, not the retrieval that fed it. A pipeline can have perfect recall and zero faithfulness. Avoid: "accuracy", "correctness" (which imply a gold answer); faithfulness is reference-free.

Atomic claim: A single verifiable statement split out of a longer answer, each scored on its own. Decomposition into atomic claims is the first step of faithfulness, because answers mix supported and unsupported pieces. Avoid: "fact" when you mean a claim that still has to be checked.

NLI (natural language inference): A model that reads a premise and a hypothesis and labels their relationship entailment (follows from), neutral (not stated), or contradiction (conflicts). In faithfulness the premise is the retrieved context and the hypothesis is the claim; only entailment counts as supported.

Answer relevance: Whether the generated answer actually addresses the question, independent of whether it is grounded. The sibling metric to faithfulness: an answer can be faithful to the context and still not answer the question asked. Avoid: "quality" when you mean only one of the two.

Where it lives

Vector store: Where chunk text and its embedding live together. For this course the default is a plain file or SQL table, not a dedicated vector database. Avoid: "vector DB" for any old place you kept a numpy array.

Embedding dimension: The fixed length of every vector an embedding model emits (e.g. 384 for all-MiniLM-L6-v2).

Ambiguities resolved in this course

"Retrieval" names the search step generally. "Dense retrieval" and "keyword search" name the two systems; "hybrid search" is their fusion. Where only one system is meant, it is named.

"Score" is always qualified by its system: cosine similarity for dense retrieval, the BM25 score for keyword search, the RRF score for a fused list.
