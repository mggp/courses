A control makes a promise

People read a control before they use it. Its label, shape, icon, position, and state predict what activation will do.

Your win: diagnose an interaction by comparing its predicted behavior with its actual behavior.

Treat the pattern as a contract

An interaction pattern is more than a component's appearance. It joins a cue to a known behavior and makes the result visible.

CueWhat the control lets a person predict.
BehaviorWhat activation changes and where.
FeedbackHow the interface exposes the result.

This gives you a useful debugging question: where does the observed interaction first depart from the prediction? A prettier control cannot repair the wrong transition.

Familiar does not mean generic

You can change color, type, spacing, and motion without discarding the learned behavior. The risky move is borrowing a familiar cue while giving it a different job.

The W3C disclosure pattern pairs a button and controlled content. A right-pointing arrow commonly means collapsed; activation reveals the content, changes the arrow, and exposes the expanded state. The visual cue, keyboard behavior, and programmatic state describe the same interaction.

Start with the user's prediction

When generated UI feels slippery, do not begin with the component library. Ask someone to predict what a control will do without activating it. Then test it. The gap between those answers is evidence.

Write the critique

“The chevron and row treatment promise an inline disclosure, but activation navigates away. Keep the editor in place, reveal the controls below the row, and expose the expanded state.”

Do this on one of your pages

  1. Choose one control whose behavior is not visible until activation.
  2. Write your prediction from its label, shape, icon, position, and state.
  3. Activate it with a pointer and keyboard. Record the change in location, content, focus, and state.
  4. If prediction and behavior differ, revise either the cue or the behavior. Keep the product requirement fixed.
